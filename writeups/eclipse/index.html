UK's 2026 Solar Eclipse & the Pinhole Effect

Yesterday the Moon covered most of the Sun over the UK and I spent part of this evening staring at a piece of card and my wall instead of the sky.

Some notes in honour of yesterday's 12th Aug 2026 eclipse in the UK, and about the pinhole thing you probably see mentioned constantly. A non-technical writing very much for fun.

What Happened

12 August 2026, early evening, the Moon swept in front of the Sun and covered somewhere around 90 to 96% of it depending where you were in the UK. London got to about 91%, Cornwall and the Isles of Scilly got closer to 96%, I got around 90% in Durham (great day to have a big West facing window on the top floor). Not a total eclipse, that stayed over at Greenland, Iceland and northern Spain, but this was the deepest partial the UK has seen since 1999, when Cornwall actually got totality. We don't get another shot at anything this good until 2090, without going abroad at least.

Bonus: the Perseids meteor shower peaked that same night, with a totally moonless sky to watch them in (since the Moon had just spent the evening in front of the Sun and was basically invisible after dark). Great night to stay outside.

The Pinhole Effect

You may have heard of the pinhole effect or a pinhole camera. Stand under a tree during a partial eclipse and the little gaps between leaves stop making round blobs of light on the ground, they start making crescents, hundreds of tiny copies of the eclipse itself. The small gaps create "pinholes" (which can be any small hole far from a surface).

I experienced this both purposefully with just a small piece of card, and accidentally while the eclipse was ending and I was having my dinner, where the gaps in my blinds projected tiny crescent images of the eclipse onto my wall for just a few moments. I was only just able to get out my phone and take a wobbly photo.

This is extremely easy to achieve, my crude and cheap setup proves that quite well... I used a toothpick to poke a tiny hole in a piece of card, and it was done.

Just by holding this manually, moving it closer then further away from a surface while it faces the eclipse directly, I achieved an (admittedly blurry) but visible demonstration and took some quick photos with my phone.

I also have a demonstration of a makeshift pinhole camera! This one was done by a friend, who similarly poked a small hole into a piece of foil, except he used it on a camera to replace the lens. This allowed him to photograph the eclipse directly, which is normally far too bright for any normal camera lens.
